Mathematics & Artificial Intelligence · Issue 8

Learned coarse spaces, boundary-consistent reduced models and proof-producing verification

Published 6 November 2026 · Curated by Hassan Mohammad

Coverage: substantive releases from 30 October–5 November 2026. Four developments met the threshold. This was a strong week for hybrid numerical methodology and inspectable verification infrastructure: learning is most convincing when it predicts a mathematically constrained component inside a classical solver, while reasoning-system gains remain smaller and less reproducible.

Reading order

  1. Learning Coarse Spaces for Two-Grid Methods — strongest numerical-method contribution.
  2. To lift or not to lift — clearest reduced-order modeling result.
  3. Sundance — strongest software and proof-artifact contribution.
  4. ReSI-Math — promising reasoning-training study with modest gains.
Numerical linear algebra · Strong theory and controlled computational evidence · Read first

1. Learning Coarse Spaces for Two-Grid Methods from Smoothed Errors with Applications to High-Contrast Porous Media Flow

Chenglong Bao, Chen Cui and Ruohua Li · arXiv · 4 November 2026 · Research paper and software artifact · Paper ↗ · Code and proofs ↗

Why it matters
Robust multilevel solvers for high-contrast media depend on coarse spaces that capture error components left behind by local smoothing. This work learns that solver-specific subspace directly, rather than learning the PDE solution map, and keeps FGMRES, finite-volume operators and local Schwarz solves explicit.

Primary contributions

  • Builds training snapshots from errors remaining after a fixed number of additive-Schwarz smoothing steps, then defines reference coarse spaces by POD in the discrete energy inner product.
  • Introduces a basis-invariant whitened subspace loss, so training targets the span of a coarse basis rather than an arbitrary choice of basis vectors.
  • Uses a continuous-coordinate generator to predict parameter-adaptive coarse spaces across discretizations, including grids substantially finer than those used for training.
  • Proves perturbation estimates for nonsymmetric two-grid operators and conditional finite-step Krylov residual-polynomial bounds without assuming stationary energy contraction.
  • Tests static-interface, moving-interface Darcy and nonlinear Forchheimer cases; on the finest reported moving-interface grid, a learned 15-dimensional space requires 20.88±0.60 FGMRES updates versus 19.12±0.60 for GenEO with an average dimension near 406.

Evidence and caution
The artifact is unusually inspectable: configurations, tests, reference summaries, reproduction instructions, an experiment-to-code map and pinned Lean sources are public. The numerical study includes POD, global POD, GenEO and DeepONet-style baselines, loss ablations and cross-grid tests. Still, the experiments are confined to two-dimensional porous-media-type problems; large projector distances can coexist with acceptable iteration counts; and the formalization covers stated algebraic assumptions, not floating-point implementations or empirical convergence. No independent reproduction is available.

Read first
Algorithm 2 for the training pipeline, Table 2 for cross-grid FGMRES results, Table 5 for nonlinear Forchheimer transfer, and Appendix C for the subspace-loss ablation. In the repository, begin with docs/reproduction.md and docs/paper_map.md.

Reduced-order modeling · Strong formulation and controlled numerics

2. To lift or not to lift: Operator inference for nonlinear PDEs with boundary control

Zhuoyuan Wang, Yorie Nakahira, Wael H. Ali and Saviz Mowlavi · arXiv · 4 November 2026 · Research paper · Paper ↗

Why it matters
Data-driven reduced models often violate time-dependent boundary conditions even when their interior trajectories look plausible. Lifting separates the exactly known boundary response from a homogeneous reduced state, giving operator inference a clean route to boundary-consistent heat, flow and transport surrogates.

Primary contributions

  • Decomposes the full state into a known boundary-satisfying lift and a homogeneous component represented in a POD basis.
  • Shows that substituting the lift into a quadratic PDE produces the bilinear state–input and quadratic input–input terms required by the reduced model.
  • Derives a nonintrusive operator-inference problem whose reconstructed state satisfies the prescribed boundary condition by construction.
  • Compares lifted and original operator inference, with intrusive and physics-informed variants, on boundary-actuated heat, Burgers and lid-driven-cavity problems.
  • Documents the important negative result that lifting is not uniformly preferable: ordinary operator inference may suffice when trajectory prediction matters more than exact boundary consistency.

Evidence and caution
The three PDE studies include multiple reduced dimensions, regularization sweeps and failure-rate reporting rather than only best-case errors. The benefit is clearest near controlled boundaries, but stability remains fragile: in the heat example, lifted-model failure rates rise from 20% to 80% as POD dimension increases from six to eight, compared with 40% to 100% for the original method. No method-specific reproduction repository or independent validation was identified, and the conclusions may not transfer beyond the tested boundary controls and quadratic formulations.

Read first
The derivation surrounding Equation 25 and Remark 3, Tables 1–3 for accuracy and failure rates, and Figure 10 for spatial error near the controlled cavity boundary.

Formal verification software · Strong artifact evidence

3. Sundance: A Simple SMT Solver for Program Verification

Amar Shah, Benjamin F. Jones, Can Cebeci, Jason Z. S. Hu, Lee A. Barnett, Rémi Delmas, Marijn Heule, Bryan Parno and Bruno Dutertre · arXiv · 3 November 2026 · Technical report and software release · Paper ↗ · Repository ↗

Why it matters
SMT solvers are increasingly the hidden trust and search layer beneath proof assistants, program verifiers and generated-proof workflows. Sundance asks whether a compact, inspectable solver specialized for verification can remain competitive while producing proof artifacts for unsatisfiable queries.

Primary contributions

  • Implements a roughly 13,600-line Rust solver combining CaDiCaL, congruence closure, linear arithmetic, algebraic datatypes and pattern-based quantifier instantiation.
  • Produces an eDRAT proof for each unsatisfiable result, connecting solver search to a separately checkable artifact.
  • Evaluates 21,531 queries drawn from Verus, Dafny and F* rather than relying on a single verification ecosystem.
  • Reports competitive aggregate coverage but substantial suite dependence: on the DICE* subset Sundance solves 25.6% versus Z3’s 96.8%, while its overall F* rate of 56.7% lies between cvc5 at 54.9% and Alt-Ergo at 59.3%.
  • Uses component-swap ablations to show that replacing individual default modules reduces solved-query count, while retaining a codebase reported to be 26–38 times smaller than Z3 or cvc5.

Evidence and caution
The public Rust implementation and broad cross-framework corpus make this a credible software result, and a held-out evaluation reduces concern that the 2,576-query Verus development set merely tuned to the final benchmark. However, Sundance is not uniformly competitive, timeout choices and portfolio effects matter, and the Valido checker was still being open-sourced at publication. Some theory lemmas are validated with external SMT solvers, so the advertised proof pipeline is not yet a wholly self-contained independently reproduced trust chain.

Read first
Section 8 and Figures 5–6 for matched evaluation and ablations, Section 9 for proof production, then the repository’s proof-checking and benchmark instructions.

Mathematical reasoning · Promising controlled method, modest effect

4. ReSI-Math: Exploring Recoverable Capability Frontiers for Efficient Reasoning Improvement

Yao Cui, Zhen Yang, Yuandong Wang, Yangfu Zhu, Tianyu Wo and Zhenzhou Shao · arXiv · 3 November 2026 · Research paper · Paper ↗ · Project page ↗

Why it matters
Reasoning fine-tuning is expensive when examples are selected by difficulty alone. ReSI-Math instead searches for failed problems whose correct solutions appear recoverable from the model’s own trajectories, then concentrates reinforcement learning on that empirical frontier.

Primary contributions

  • Uses a separately trained self-judge and evolutionary trajectory crossover to identify failed mathematical problems that can yield verifier-confirmed correct offspring.
  • Recovers 272 correct offspring from 621 crossover attempts, an author-reported 43.8% yield, and uses those outcomes to define a recoverability-based training frontier.
  • Applies policy-adaptive one-problem sequential RL with verifiable rewards to Qwen3-VL-8B rather than collecting a large static curriculum.
  • Across three sequential stages, raises the reported MATH500/WE-MATH average from 78.71 to 80.16 while monitoring changes in judge behavior.
  • Includes a matched single-problem selection ablation: the frontier-selected problem reaches 54.16 overall versus 53.31 for a random problem, a modest 0.85-point advantage.

Evidence and caution
The crossover accounting, sequential traces and selection ablation are useful controls, but “capability frontier” is an operational construct rather than a demonstrated intrinsic boundary. Results center on the Qwen3-VL family and mathematical benchmarks; solver and judge are optimized separately; the deterministic verifier has no independent validation; and no public implementation was identified. The effect is directionally interesting but too small and narrow to support claims of a generally efficient reasoning curriculum.

Read first
Table 3 for the matched selection ablation, Figure 3 for sequential policy dynamics, and Table 6 plus the verifier description before accepting the headline frontier claim.

Signal versus hype

The strongest evidence favors hybrid systems with explicit numerical or logical structure: learned coarse spaces inside FGMRES, analytic boundary lifts inside operator inference, and proof-producing search inside a compact SMT solver. These results improve identifiable components while preserving classical residual checks, boundary constraints or proof artifacts. They do not establish that learned preconditioners generalize across PDE classes, that lifting always improves reduced models, that a small SMT solver dominates mature engines, or that recoverability-guided RL reveals a universal mathematical-reasoning frontier.
